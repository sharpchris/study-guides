1Study outline

The main ideas of the lesson, in order. Vocabulary words are highlighted.

I.What was the Renaissance?

  • The Renaissance was a golden age in the arts and learning in Western Europe from the 1300s to the 1500s. The word means “rebirth.”
    • It brought big changes in economics, politics, and society, and above all in culture.
    • It was the bridge between medieval times and early modern times.
    • Growing cities and trade opened up people’s view of the world.
  • A new worldview
    • Through contact with Spain and Sicily, Christian scholars studied Muslim philosophy, art, and science. Muslim scholars also passed along knowledge of ancient Greece and Rome.
    • Renewed interest in Greek and Roman learning inspired people to remake their own age. They saw it as a rebirth after the disorder of the Middle Ages.
    • It wasn’t a total break from the past: Latin and ancient thinkers like Euclid and Aristotle had survived the Middle Ages.
    • The big shift: medieval scholars focused on life after death, while Renaissance thinkers wanted to explore life here and now.
    • New emphasis on individual achievement. The Renaissance ideal was a person talented in many different fields.
  • A spirit of adventure and curiosity
    • Navigators explored unknown seas, and scientists studied the universe in new ways.
    • Italian thinker Pico della Mirandola expressed this confidence in human ability: people can become whatever they choose.
  • Humanism: the intellectual movement at the heart of the Renaissance
    • Humanists studied classical Greek and Roman cultures to better understand their own times.
    • Most were devout Christians, but they focused on secular (worldly) subjects instead of religious ones.
    • They believed education should build a person’s creativity, and stressed the humanities: grammar, rhetoric (using language effectively), poetry, and history.
  • Francesco Petrarch, an early humanist from Florence (1300s)
    • Tracked down old Greek and Roman manuscripts in monasteries and churches, which helped bring back works by Cicero, Homer, Virgil, and Livy.
    • Wrote love poems, the Sonnets to Laura, that influenced other writers.
    • Wrote in the vernacular (the everyday language of ordinary people) as well as in Latin.

II.Why the Renaissance began in Italy

  • It began in Italy in the mid-1300s, spread north to the rest of Europe, and peaked in the 1500s.
  • History and geography
    • Italy had been the center of ancient Rome. Ruins, statues, coins, and inscriptions were everyday reminders of Rome’s glory.
  • Thriving cities
    • Northern city-states like Florence, Milan, Venice, and Genoa had grown rich from trade and manufacturing during the Middle Ages. Rome and Naples also contributed.
  • Trade with the Muslim world
    • Ships brought goods, people, and ideas to Italy’s ports. The Muslim world had preserved Greek and Roman learning, so many lost texts were recovered this way.
  • A wealthy merchant class
    • Rich merchants held political and economic power, valued individual achievement, and spent heavily on the arts.
  • Florence and the Medici
    • Florence became the symbol of the Renaissance, producing many poets, artists, architects, scholars, and scientists in a short time, much like ancient Athens.
    • The Medici family built a banking business in the 1400s, then expanded into manufacturing and mining. Their money became political and cultural power.
    • Cosimo de’ Medici took control of Florence’s government in 1434, and the family ruled the city for years without a crown.
    • Lorenzo de’ Medici (“the Magnificent”), Cosimo’s grandson, was a skilled politician and a generous patron (financial supporter) of the arts. Artists practiced by sketching Roman statues in the Medici gardens.

III.Art flourishes

  • Wealthy patrons, including popes, princes, and merchants, supported hundreds of artists.
  • Art reflected humanist ideas
    • Religious figures like Mary and Jesus were still painted, but often with Greek or Roman backgrounds.
    • Portraits of real people showed the interest in individuals. Artists also painted Greek and Roman myths and historical events.
    • Donatello made a life-size statue of a soldier on horseback, the first since ancient times.
  • New techniques made art more realistic
    • Perspective: showing a three-dimensional world on a flat surface by making faraway objects smaller, creating a sense of depth.
    • Shading with light and dark to make objects look round and real.
    • Studying anatomy and drawing from live models to show the human body accurately.
  • Architecture
    • Architects rejected the medieval Gothic style as disorderly and brought back Greek and Roman columns, arches, and domes.
    • Filippo Brunelleschi built the huge dome on Florence’s cathedral (the Duomo), modeled on the Pantheon in Rome. He was also an engineer who invented machines to build it.
  • Leonardo da Vinci (born 1452)
    • Endlessly curious inventor and artist. He sketched nature and studied bodies to learn how bones and muscles work.
    • Painted the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper, a great example of perspective.
    • Interested in botany, anatomy, optics, music, architecture, and engineering. His notebooks show flying machines and underwater boats centuries before they existed.
  • Michelangelo (born 1475)
    • Sculptor, painter, architect, engineer, and poet, supported by the Medici.
    • Sculpted the Pietà (Mary holding Jesus) and David, which echoes ancient Greek sculpture.
    • Starting in 1508, spent about four years painting Bible scenes on the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in Rome.
    • Designed the dome of St. Peter’s in Rome, a model for later buildings like the U.S. Capitol.
  • Raphael
    • Younger than Leonardo and Michelangelo, he studied their work and blended Christian and classical styles.
    • School of Athens shows great thinkers like Plato, Aristotle, Socrates, and Averroës, and includes Michelangelo, Leonardo, and Raphael himself.

IV.New books reflect Renaissance themes

  • “How-to” books helped ambitious people succeed at the courts of Renaissance rulers.
  • Baldassare Castiglione, The Book of the Courtier
    • Described the manners, skills, learning, and virtues a member of the court should have.
    • The ideal man is athletic, plays music, and knows literature and history without being arrogant. The ideal woman is graceful, kind, and lively but reserved.
  • Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince (1513)
    • A Florentine diplomat who had watched kings and princes up close and studied Roman history.
    • A guide for rulers on how to gain and keep power, based on how real rulers actually behaved, not on high ideals as Plato had written.
    • Argued that the end justifies the means: rulers should do whatever is necessary to reach their goals.
    • Critics called it cynical, and Machiavellian came to mean using deceit in politics. Others say it was a realistic look at politics, and it still raises debate about power and government.
